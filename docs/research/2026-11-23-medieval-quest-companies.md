# How medieval companies took their work

*Research report, 2026-11-23. Page: [How medieval companies took their work](https://cursor.com/canvas/store-77423492-0240-4ade-b23c-c31d2a9643f0/e33da840-3a2b-47a0-a10a-d8ef0c0b255d). Applied 2026-11-24: Option A (the Eighth Day writ-board) as the core model, with light touches of B, C, and D (D-00215). Findings unchanged; see [README.md](README.md) for status.*

A reference for Outremer: why small armed groups formed to take outside jobs in the Middle Ages, where those contracts actually came from, and four lore options for clans and the town mission board. Facts I could not confirm, or that scholars dispute, are flagged.

## The short version
Small armed groups did not form in the Middle Ages because adventuring was a career. They formed because kings, towns, and military orders could not cover every road, raid, and frontier with their own standing men. Local scouts, term-serving knights, sworn town brotherhoods, and hired companies filled the gaps.

The work those groups took was not a rumor in a tavern. It was a written commission or a public cry: a sealed writ from a castellan, an abbey, a bishop, a town council, or a military order, proclaimed by a crier at the market, the church door, the market cross, or the gate, and sometimes posted there in writing. Payment was wages, an advance on a contract, a share of recovered goods, a local levy, or, for lay associates of an order, spiritual privileges as much as coin.

For Outremer, the closest historical fit for Dismas is not a generic adventurers' guild and not a full monastic brotherhood. It is an independent sworn company that takes sealed writs from the Order of the Eighth Day and from towns, the way turcopoles and term-serving knights served the Templars and Hospitallers without taking vows. The town mission board has a real ancestor in those public cries and posted notices. Make the board feel like a legal instrument, not a pub listing. Pitches and trade-offs are at the end.

## Military-order auxiliaries
The military orders of the Crusader states never had enough professed brothers to hold the frontier alone. Frankish manpower, horses, and heavy armor were scarce and expensive. The standing answer was to hire or associate men who were not monks.

### Turcopoles
Turcopoles were locally recruited light cavalry and mounted archers employed by the Byzantine Empire, the Crusader states, and the military orders. The name comes from Greek *tourkopouloi*, "sons of Turks." Crusaders first met them in Byzantine service on the First Crusade, where some contemporary writers (Raymond of Aguilers, Albert of Aachen) describe men of mixed Turkic and Christian parentage.

In the Latin East the word quickly became a job more than a bloodline. Many turcopoles were probably recruited from Syrian Orthodox Christians and Christianized Turks; by the later twelfth century some recorded names are Poulains, Frankish settlers who had stayed after 1099. Westerners trained as mounted archers may have been included as well. There is no solid evidence that Frankish turcopoles were typically of mixed heritage; that description belongs mainly to the Byzantine units. By the thirteenth century, as Jonathan Riley-Smith and others have argued, the term referred to function rather than race.

They rode lighter, faster horses, wore quilted armor rather than a knight's full kit, and used bows and light lances. William of Tyre calls them "light-armed horsemen." Hospitaller regulations distinguish the heavy war saddles of brother-knights from the "Turkish saddles" issued to Syrian turcopoles. Their value was scouting, raiding, and skirmishing, work in which heavy Frankish cavalry was at a disadvantage. On those expeditions, a Turcopolier could give orders to accompanying knights, the one known case of that reversal.

The numbers show how much the orders relied on them. In 1168 the Hospitallers pledged 500 turcopoles to serve beside 500 knight-brothers on an expedition into Muslim territory. A later planning figure for 1186 makes turcopoles half of a 1,000-man Hospitaller contingent. Unlike unsalaried brother-knights and brother-sergeants, turcopoles were paid warriors, and they ate at a separate table from the other mounted soldiers of the Temple or the Hospital. Paying them was one reason cash had to be sent from Europe to the Latin East. A Latin source for the Battle of Hattin in 1187 gives the Christian army 4,000 turcopoles; Steven Runciman thought that figure exaggerated. Those captured were reportedly executed, treated as renegades.

### Confratres, donats, sergeants, and service ad terminum
Beside paid auxiliaries, the orders kept a ring of lay associates who were not fully professed. Jochen Schenk's study of the Temple treats three forms as connected rather than tidy separate ranks: the *confrater*, the *donatus*, and the *miles ad terminum*.

A confrater was a lay "brother" or sister who kept a secular life but was received into a spiritual association with the order: a share in its prayers and good works, often the right to burial in its cemetery, and sometimes its protection, in return for gifts of property, income, or equipment. A surviving grant of 1209 shows Count William of Forcalquier received *in donatum et confratrem*: he promised his armor, horses, and a hundred marks at death, plus a yearly gift, and pledged to take no other religious habit if he ever professed.

Donats became, around the turn of the thirteenth century, the most prominent form of this association, in part as canon law after Lateran III and IV tightened older confraternity practice. Alan Forey notes that some associates served without pay, and that a Hospitaller statute of 1292 allowed donats to be recruited freely in Spain because it was a frontier, which implies military use. Knights also served *ad terminum*, for a fixed term, a practice as old as the Temple itself. Popes granted indulgences to men who helped guard frontier castles of Calatrava, the Swordbrethren, or Alcántara for a season. Schenk argues that donats and temporary knights could function as a novitiate in disguise once formal Templar novitiates declined; that is his reading, not a settled fact.

Brother-sergeants were different again: they were professed members of the order, not hired hands, cheaper than knights but bound by vows. The Templar Turcopolier commanded both the mercenary cavalry raised in the East and the sergeant-brothers. The useful distinction for Outremer is the one the orders already made: professed brothers on one side, and on the other a mixed body of paid locals and term-serving laymen who fought under the order's writ without becoming monks.

## Road brotherhoods and peace leagues

### Hermandades
In medieval Castile, a *hermandad* ("brotherhood") was a sworn union of towns, formed first in the twelfth century, at first temporary and later permanent. The usual aims were to police roads and open country and to resist violent magnates. Mounted constables, the *cuadrilleros*, hunted bandits and rural criminals and could both seize and summarily try suspects. The crown at first disliked them; Henry II accepted and regulated them by decree in 1370. Similar leagues appear in Aragon from the mid-thirteenth century, when councils formed juntas to keep the roads safe, later headed by royal *sobrejunteros*.

The frontier is the point. Outlaws used the edges between kingdoms as a refuge. Towns therefore swore across borders: Salvatierra with Navarrese villages in 1293, and a Guipuzcoan-Navarrese hermandad in 1329, funded by a special tribute. The later Santa Hermandad of 1476, created at Madrigal under the Catholic Monarchs, is better documented than the early leagues: each place chose *alcaldes*, *cuadrilleros* answered a hue sounded on the bells, and pursuit ran about five leagues before the next district took up the chase. Their brief was crimes in waste or deserted ground: highway robbery, rustling, killing, and the burning of houses and crops. That late ordinance should not be read back word for word onto the twelfth century, but the pattern is older: towns pool armed men because no single jurisdiction owns the road.

### Peace of God, pilgrimage roads, and merchant escorts
The Peace of God, first proclaimed at the Council of Charroux in 989, used excommunication to protect churches, agricultural resources, and unarmed clerics. The Truce of God, proclaimed at Toulouges in 1027, tried to limit the days and seasons of noble violence. The movement had popular support and sometimes raised a militia: in 1038 Archbishop Aimo of Bourges swore clergy and unarmed laypeople into a peace league and led them against oath-breakers, a campaign that ended badly. Pilgrims were among those the peace statutes aimed to protect.

Armed escort of sacred or commercial travel is well attested in other forms. The Temple itself was founded around 1119 to protect pilgrims on the road to Jerusalem. Relic translations could travel under armed guard; at Bari in 1087, armed detachments watched the relics of St. Nicholas day and night. From the mid-fourteenth century, Hanseatic merchants sailed in convoys with armed escort vessels, the *vredenschepe* or "peace ships." Italian *compagnia*, from Late Latin *companio*, "one who shares bread," could mean a commercial partnership or, as in Genoa's Compagna, a sworn civic association bound by oath to mutual support. *Consorterie*, by contrast, were mainly patrilineal kin groups around a shared tower or property, not mercenary bands, and should not be stretched into a clan analogue.

## Free companies and the condotta
From the twelfth century, bands of professional soldiers appear in Western wars under names such as *brabançons*, *routiers*, and later *compagnies d'aventure* or *grandes compagnies*. Many formed when a peace left fighting men unpaid and far from home: the Catalan Company after the Peace of Caltabellotta in 1302, and the free companies in France after the Treaty of Brétigny in 1360. Between contracts they lived by plunder and extortion. Siena paid companies off 37 times, spending hundreds of thousands of florins in the later fourteenth century. That record is a warning, not a model to copy for Dismas.

What is worth copying is the paperwork and the reason outsiders banded together. A *condotta* was a written contract, registered by a state's chancery, naming how many lances, horses, and foot a captain would supply, for how long, at what pay, with rules for muster and inspection, and a split of plunder and ransoms. Pay often began with a *prestanza*, an advance, then came in installments. Late pay was the usual cause of mutiny or defection. Some contracts included a *menda* clause, compensation for lost or ruined horses and kit. Englishmen in the White Company had a written clause that they need not fight other Englishmen. Companies kept treasurers, notaries, and chancellors. Early Italian terms were often only a season or a few weeks; later states tried to keep captains on longer, more controlled retainers.

The companies themselves were routinely mixed. The White Company, which entered Italy in 1361 under Albert Sterz and later John Hawkwood, was called English but included Germans, French, Italians, and Hungarians, veterans of the wars in France. It was organized in lances of three, grouped under corporals who were often sub-contractors. The Catalan Company, led by Roger de Flor, was hired by Emperor Andronikos II in 1303 to fight in Anatolia. It was built from almogavar companies, themselves groups of some 20 to 50 men following a proven chief, bundled into a "company of companies." Troop numbers are disputed: Ramon Muntaner and some later writers give about 8,000 men on departure, while the Byzantine historian Nikephoros Gregoras gives only about 1,000.

Italian city-states used these companies because a small population made a year-round standing army hard to maintain, the same manpower logic that sent the military orders looking for turcopoles. The difference is moral tone. Condottieri were contractors. Some, like Hawkwood in later service, became relatively disciplined retainers; many were extortionists with a seal. For Outremer, take the written terms, the mixed company, and the advance-and-share pay. Leave the peacetime ransoming of towns to rival or fallen clans, if at all.

## Where the jobs were posted
Work arrived as a document or a cry, not as a casual notice in an inn. Officials issued written writs and commissions: a bailiff, a sheriff, a castellan, a bishop, an abbot, or a town council named the task, the authority, and often the pay or the share. The holder carried a sealed letter that authorized the act. That is the historical core of a "quest."

In a low-literacy world the first publication was oral. Across medieval Europe, sworn town criers proclaimed ordinances, summonses, market rules, and other official messages at marketplaces, halls, crossroads, gates, and churches. Written notices were then affixed at the same ordinary places so the cry would still be there after the crier had gone: church doors, market crosses, guildhalls, and gatehouses. Church doors and porches were civic as well as sacred sites, used for public business and, in later medieval England, for bills and notices. The richest evidence for criers nailing up placards is late medieval and early modern. Do not claim that twelfth-century Outremer already had a wall of paper jobs; do use the older, well-attested pairing of a sealed writ plus a public cry at the church, the market, or the gate.

Two related instruments need care. Letters of marque and reprisal, attested in Mediterranean and French practice in the thirteenth century and in an English statute of 1354, were licenses to recover a proven loss from the subjects of another power, usually in peacetime, up to the value of the injury. Wartime privateering commissions are later. They are a good flavor for a sealed license to act, not a model for a general job board. Likewise, medieval England had no standard cash bounty-hunter market. Hue and cry obliged the community to pursue a felon; outlawry made a fugitive seizable by anyone; special commissions could be issued to investigate violent crime. Castile's hermandades are the closer analogue to posted road work with organized pursuers. Monasteries and shrines also commissioned escorts for pilgrims, relic translations, and the defense of sacred ground, paid or privileged by the house that issued the request.

## What this means for Outremer
What we already have: the Order of the Eighth Day posts jobs and holds holy strongholds ([WORLD.md](../WORLD.md), [STORY.md](../STORY.md)); squads work as FFTA2-style clans (D-00201); Dismas leads a mixed-race quest-taking company, not only a hunting party (D-00214); and town boards offer missions, shops, and travel on a fixed node map (D-00045, D-00210). The lore has to explain all four without turning the board into a generic adventurers' guild, and without pre-locking the Order's founding event or later story secrets.

### Takeaways
1. **Companies exist because the Order and the towns are thin on the ground.** Professed brothers hold the strongholds; they cannot also walk every road, trace every missing patrol, or escort every shrine procession. That is why they rely on independent companies, the way the Temple and the Hospital relied on turcopoles and term-serving knights.
2. **A clan in Outremer should feel like a sworn company, not a family and not the Order itself.** People of different races can join by oath, term, or pay, which already fits FFTA2 recruiting and Dismas's Cynocephalus and Hoopoe-kin companions. Old-way or convert recruits can be hired hands, term-servers, or fuller companions without all becoming Eighth Day monastics.
3. **Quests should look like writs.** A job on the town board is a sealed commission, cried in the market and posted at the church door, the market cross, or the Order's gatehouse. The Eighth Day is the principal poster, especially for patrols, escorts, and holy-ground work, but town councils, castellans, and abbeys can post too. That keeps the board varied without inventing a secular guild.
4. **Pay can be an advance, a completion fee, a share of recovered goods or gear, or the Order's hospitality and protection for the term.** Bigger commissions can carry written terms, a muster, and a time limit, echoing a condotta without making the company a band of extortionists.
5. **Use the darker free-company pattern sparingly.** Rival clans who take writs and then prey on the roads when unpaid, or who sell their seals to the highest bidder, give the board moral texture. They should not be the player's default identity.

### Option A: The Eighth Day writ-board
Towns keep a public board at the church door, the market cross, or the Order gatehouse. Sealed writs are cried and posted there. The Order of the Eighth Day is the principal poster: missing patrols, road escorts, recoveries, and the defense of holy ground. Town councils, castellans, and abbeys can post as well. Independent sworn companies take a writ, carry the seal on the road, fulfill it, and collect the posted pay or share. Companies form because the Order cannot garrison every road, and mixed-race locals know the wilds better than many professed brothers.
- Pros: fits Dismas, the Eighth Day, town boards, and mixed recruiting without a generic guild; the missing-patrol quest is already a writ from the Order; leaves room for abbey, town, and later side-mission posters.
- Cons: needs a little in-world vocabulary so the board does not read as a tavern listing; has to stay clear that companies are not themselves the Order.

### Option B: Conduct-companies
Every clan is a formal contractor. Taking a job means signing a short written condotta with a sponsor, usually the Order or a town: term, headcount, pay, advance, shares, and a finish date. Clean systems fit, and accept-or-decline recruiting maps naturally onto a captain adding names to a muster roll.
- Pros: strong paperwork flavor and clear pay rules; good later use for large or multi-part commissions.
- Cons: historical companies often extorted and plundered between contracts; can feel like a mercenary office, which fights the heroic tone.

### Option C: Road brotherhoods
Towns and the Order jointly swear a hermandad-style peace league. Clans are the armed arms of that league. They patrol roads, hunt outlaws and Fiends, and answer a public cry when a writ is posted or a bell is rung. Chapter 1's hunters' town would feel like a frontier brotherhood from the first map.
- Pros: excellent civic and holy-road flavor for Chapter 1; explains work that happens between jurisdictions, out on the nodes.
- Cons: weaker fit for random generics and named wanderers who join from outside the league; can make every job feel like local police work.

### Option D: Lay brethren of the Eighth Day
Playable clans are confraternities attached to the Order: confratres and donats who share in its prayers and protection, serve for a term, and take its writs, but do not take monastic vows. Closest to the Templar analogue, and a clean answer to why a mixed company may carry the Eighth Day's seal.
- Pros: closest Crusades-order flavor; keeps Tristan's Templar vows distinct from a clan oath.
- Cons: blurs the clan with the Order, especially once Aveline is serving the Eighth Day; old-way or reluctant recruits are harder to justify.

### Recommendation (not locked)
Take Option A as the default. Let a company be known as a friend or term-servant of the Eighth Day, a light touch of Option D, without making the clan a branch of the Order. Use Option B's written terms on larger or multi-part commissions, including Chapter 1's two-node patrol. Keep Option C as flavor for the starting town and its roads, not as the rule for every recruit. Skip a pure mercenary-guild or pure monastic reading.
